# Handouts, Worksheets, and Printables

Slides disappear the moment the talk ends. Handouts stay. The worksheet a student takes home, the flyer a family pins to the fridge, the one-pager a buyer slides across the table — these do work for you after you've left the room.

This lesson is about making one-page documents that look like someone actually thought about them. AI helps with the words, the layout ideas, and even the images — but a printable has constraints a slide doesn't, and that's what we'll focus on.

---

## The tools we'll use

- [https://www.canva.com](https://www.canva.com) — the main tool. Free tier is enough. Its "Magic Design," "Magic Write," and AI image features are all built in.
- [https://claude.ai](https://claude.ai) or [https://chatgpt.com](https://chatgpt.com) — for writing the text first.
- [https://ideogram.ai](https://ideogram.ai) — only for graphics with text on them (poster headers).
- [https://www.adobe.com/express](https://www.adobe.com/express) — alternative to Canva, similar AI features.

Canva is where you'll spend most of your time. Get a free account, then come back.

---

## Step 1: Write the content before you open Canva

If you open Canva first, you'll spend 40 minutes picking a template and 5 minutes on what it actually says. Do it the other way around.

Write the whole handout as plain text in Claude or ChatGPT first. One page = roughly 250–400 words of body text, plus a headline and a clear next step.

Copy this prompt:

```
Help me draft the text for a one-page handout.

Audience: [who]
Purpose: [what they should know or do after reading]
Tone: [warm / professional / urgent / playful]

Structure:
- A short headline (max 6 words)
- One sentence that says what this is
- 3 to 5 short sections with mini-headings
- A clear "what to do next" at the bottom

Keep sentences short. No filler. No "we are excited to."
```

Read the result out loud before you put it into Canva. If a section sounds like a press release, rewrite it.

---

## Step 2: Three flavors of a one-pager

The same structure works for very different things:

- **Teacher — worksheet:** A reading comprehension worksheet for Year 4. Headline, a short paragraph to read, 4 questions, a space for the student's name. Black and white, will be photocopied.
- **NGO worker — event flyer:** A flyer for a community vaccination day. Headline, date and location in big text, 3 short bullets of what to bring, a phone number to call. Color, printed on A5.
- **Business — one-page tearsheet:** A product one-pager for a sales meeting. Headline, one-sentence pitch, 3 benefits, one chart or stat, and contact details at the bottom. Brand colors, printed and PDF.

Notice the differences: the worksheet has to leave room for handwriting, the flyer has to read from 2 meters away, the tearsheet has to survive being emailed as a PDF. The content drives the layout — not the other way around.

## Step 3: Build it in Canva

Once your text is ready:

1. Go to [canva.com](https://www.canva.com), click "Create a design" → pick the right size. A4 for most handouts. A5 for flyers. US Letter if you're in the US.
2. Search templates by purpose ("worksheet," "flyer," "one-pager"). Pick one that's *close enough* — don't waste 20 minutes hunting for perfect.
3. Replace the dummy text with your text from Step 1. All of it.
4. Replace stock images with images that match your topic — either generated from the previous lesson, or photos you actually have.
5. Use "Magic Resize" if you need the same handout in A4, A5, and Instagram square.

Canva's "Magic Write" can also rephrase text inside the design, but it tends to add filler. Trust the text you already wrote.

---

## Step 4: Layout basics that AI won't fix for you

A template doesn't make a layout good. Two rules will fix 90% of bad handouts:

**One. Pick three font sizes and stick to them.**
A big one for the headline (around 36pt). A medium one for section headings (around 18pt). A small one for body text (10–12pt). That's it. Every time you add a fourth size, the page gets noisier.

**Two. Leave white space.**
Beginners cram every corner. A handout that breathes is easier to read than one that doesn't. If the page feels full, cut something.

If you want a second opinion, paste a screenshot of your handout back into Claude:

```
Look at this handout. Tell me:
1. Can a person read the most important information in 5 seconds?
2. Is there a clear hierarchy — headline, sections, body?
3. What's the one thing I should change?

Do not redesign it. Just tell me.
```

## Step 5: Print vs screen — they are different

A handout that looks great on your laptop can print awful. A few real differences:

- **Colors shift in print.** That vivid neon green on screen prints muddy. Test print one copy before you print 200.
- **Light grey text is unreadable on paper.** Use real black for body text.
- **Photos need higher resolution for print.** AI-generated images at default resolution can look fuzzy on paper. Generate at the highest resolution your tool offers, or upscale before printing.
- **Margins matter.** Most printers can't print to the edge. Keep important text at least 1cm from the edge of the page.
- **PDF is the only safe sharing format.** Word and PowerPoint files re-flow on the recipient's computer. Export from Canva as "PDF for print" or "PDF standard."

For documents people will *only* see on a phone (a WhatsApp flyer, an Instagram post), forget about printing — use color freely and design for vertical screens.

---

## What AI gets wrong with handouts

- **It doesn't know paper sizes.** It will happily suggest a layout that won't fit on an A4 page.
- **It over-designs.** AI templates love gradients, shapes, and decorative elements. For a serious tearsheet or worksheet, plainer is better.
- **Text in generated images is still broken.** If a poster has your organization's name as part of the image, generate the background separately and put the text on top in Canva.
- **It will not catch a typo in your phone number.** Triple-check anything someone will use to contact you.
- **Brand consistency is your job.** AI does not know your colors or your fonts unless you tell it, and even then it forgets across sessions.

Rule of thumb: **AI drafts the text and suggests the look. You set the constraints — size, fonts, margins, brand.** The boring decisions are where handouts get good.

## Homework

1. Pick one handout you'll need in the next two weeks (a worksheet, a flyer, a one-pager). Use the Step 1 prompt to draft the text in Claude or ChatGPT.
2. Build it in [canva.com](https://www.canva.com) using a template. Apply the "three font sizes" rule and the "leave white space" rule.
3. Export as PDF and print one copy. Look at it on actual paper. Note one thing that looked fine on screen but doesn't on paper.
4. Optional: take a screenshot of your handout and run the Step 4 review prompt. Make the one change it suggests.
