# Planning Career Growth

Most career planning happens by accident. A friend mentions an opportunity. A boss leaves and a door opens. A bad week makes you start scrolling job listings at midnight. Then five years pass.

AI doesn't replace the slow, real work of figuring out what you actually want. But it's surprisingly good at the parts we usually skip: naming the gaps, drawing the map, drafting the awkward documents, stress-testing the decision.

This final lesson pulls together everything from the previous three: AI as tutor, AI as critic, AI as practice partner — all aimed at one question. *Where do I want to be in a year, and what do I have to do to get there?*

---

## The Posture Shift

Most people use AI to polish their résumé. That's fine. It's not what we're doing here.

We're going to use AI as a mentor who already knows where you want to go. Someone who can ask the hard questions, map the unknowns, and help you stress-test a choice.

A real mentor would do this in 4 hours of conversation across 6 months. AI can do a thinner version in an afternoon. The thin version is much better than nothing.

Tools we'll use:

- [claude.ai](https://claude.ai) — best for long-form planning and document drafting
- [chatgpt.com](https://chatgpt.com) — best for back-and-forth and the voice debrief
- [gemini.google.com](https://gemini.google.com) — useful for researching specific roles, salaries, industries

## Demo: Deciding Between Two Job Paths

Universal example for the lesson: you have two real options in front of you (or could in the next year). Option A is staying in your current track and going for the next promotion. Option B is jumping sideways to a related but different role somewhere new. You don't know which is right.

We'll walk through four uses of AI: gap analysis, the 6-month and 1-year plan, the mentor conversation, and the decision draft.

---

## Use 1: Mapping the Skill Gaps

Before you can plan, you have to know what's missing. Be honest with AI here — it can only work with what you tell it.

```
I'm considering two career paths over the next 12-24 months:

Path A: stay in my current role, push for promotion to [next level]
Path B: move into [different role] at a new company

Here's what I currently do well: [list 4-6 things]
Here's what I think I'm weak at: [list 3-4 things]
Here's the role I had 2 years ago vs now: [brief]

For each path, tell me:
1. The top 3 skills I'd need to build that I currently don't have
2. The top 2 weaknesses that would actively hold me back
3. One skill I'm probably overrating in myself

Be specific. Don't list generic things like "leadership."
Push back if I'm being vague.
```

The "push back if I'm being vague" line matters. Without it, AI will work with whatever sloppy self-assessment you give it.

---

## Use 2: The 6-Month and 1-Year Plan

Once you have the gap map, build the plan. This is where AI is genuinely useful — it can hold a lot of detail and structure it cleanly.

```
Based on the gaps we just identified for Path A, build me a plan with:

6-month plan:
- 2-3 concrete skills to build (with how I'll build them)
- 2-3 visible things to ship at work that demonstrate these skills
- 1 relationship to build deliberately
- What I should be able to point to in a review at month 6

1-year plan:
- Where I should be positioned for the promotion conversation
- The 2-3 results my boss would need to see
- What I should stop doing to make room

Be honest about how much can actually fit in 6 months. Most people
plan 2x too much. Cut the plan to what's real.
```

Repeat the same prompt for Path B. Now you have two real plans you can compare side by side instead of two vague feelings.

## What to Learn vs What to Do

A trap in early-career planning: thinking growth means more courses, more certificates, more learning. Usually that's wrong.

Ask AI directly:

```
Looking at my 6-month plan, what % of my time should be "learning"
(courses, reading, study) vs "doing" (shipping work, taking on harder
projects, getting visible reps)?

For someone at my stage and these goals, what's the right ratio,
and which specific items in my plan are learning vs doing?
```

The answer for most working professionals is something like 20/80 — way more doing than learning. AI will usually push you the right way here if you ask.

## Use 3: The Mentor Who Already Knows Where You Want To Go

This is the most powerful prompt in the lesson. Take it slowly.

```
I want you to play a mentor I'm meeting for the first time.

You've already been told, by a mutual friend, where I want to be
in 3 years: [describe in one sentence].

You've seen 50 people try to get there. Some made it, some didn't.
You know the pattern of who makes it.

You're going to ask me the questions a real mentor would ask before
giving any advice. Don't give advice yet. Ask me 5-7 hard questions,
one at a time, waiting for my answer before moving to the next.

The questions should be the ones I'm avoiding.

Start.
```

Answer honestly. Don't perform. The questions you flinch at are the ones worth sitting with.

After the questions, ask:

```
Drop the role. Based on what I just told you, what's the honest
read? Where am I being unrealistic? What's the version of this
plan that would actually work?
```

## Use 4: Drafting a Personal Review

End of year, mid-year, or before a big conversation with your boss: write your own review first. Use AI to draft it, then tear it apart.

```
Help me draft a self-review covering the last 12 months.

Wins: [list 5-8]
Things I'm proud of beyond the wins: [list 2-3]
What I didn't do well: [be honest, list 3-4]
What I'm taking responsibility for that wasn't fully my fault: [optional]

Write it in my voice — direct, not corporate, not falsely modest.
About one page. End with what I want to focus on in the next 12 months.
```

Then use the critique posture from lesson 2:

```
Now read it as a skeptical manager. What sounds like spin?
Where am I overclaiming? What did I leave out that this manager
would notice was missing?
```

## Deciding Between Two Paths

When the plans are built and the gaps mapped, you still have to choose. AI is useful for the structure of the decision, not the decision itself.

```
I have Path A and Path B in front of me. Here's what each looks like
in 1 year and 3 years based on the plans we built: [paste]

Don't tell me which to pick. Instead:
1. What's the question I haven't asked yet about each path?
2. What information would I need to make this decision with confidence?
3. What's the regret risk on each path — the version of me who picks
   this path and regrets it, what would they say?
```

Sit with the regret-risk answer. It's often the most useful frame for a hard decision.

---

## What AI Doesn't Know

It doesn't know your industry's politics — who's rising, who's stalling, which leaders are about to leave.

It doesn't know your network — who would take your call, who owes you a favor, who'd quietly block you.

It doesn't know your specific manager — how she actually makes promotion decisions, what she values that she doesn't say out loud.

It doesn't know your life — your family situation, your health, what you can actually sustain.

For all of these, you need real humans. A mentor. A peer in your field. Honest friends like the ones in this room.

The honest stack: use AI to do the prep work and stress-test the thinking. Use people for the parts only people can do.

## Homework

1. Run the gap-mapping prompt on one real career path you're considering. Be ruthlessly honest about the weakness list — that's the whole point.

2. Build the 6-month plan. Then cut it in half. Whatever's left is probably what's actually doable.

3. Run the mentor role-play. Sit with the questions for at least a day before responding to AI's read. Bring your favorite question to the next session.

4. Identify one real human — not in this room — you'll ask to mentor or advise you on this path in the next 30 days. AI got you ready. Now go find the person.
