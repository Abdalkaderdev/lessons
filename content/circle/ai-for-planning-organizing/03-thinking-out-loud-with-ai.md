# Thinking Out Loud with AI

Most people use AI like a search engine. Ask a question, get an answer, done. That's fine for facts. It's not the most interesting use.

The more useful mode is using AI as a thinking partner. Not to give you the answer — to help you find your own. You talk through a decision, it asks questions, you notice what you actually believe.

This lesson is about that mode.

---

## Why this works

When you keep a worry in your head, it goes in circles. The moment you say it out loud — to a friend, a therapist, even into a voice memo — it starts to take shape. You hear what you're really saying.

AI gives you a 24-hour version of that. It listens, reflects back, asks follow-ups. It doesn't get tired or judge you for circling the same point three times.

It's not magic. It's just a useful container for your own thinking.

## Tools we'll use

- [claude.ai](https://claude.ai) — generally feels more conversational
- [chatgpt.com](https://chatgpt.com) — also works, slightly more directive by default
- Voice mode in either app is excellent for this — talking is faster than typing

Try voice mode at least once during this lesson. It changes the experience.

## The "help me think" prompt

```
I want to think through a decision out loud. I don't want you to give me
the answer. Your job is to ask me questions that help me get clearer.

The decision is: [describe it].
What I'm leaning toward: [your gut answer].
What's making me hesitate: [the real reason you haven't decided].

Start by asking me one question. Just one. Wait for my answer
before asking the next.
```

The "just one" instruction matters. Without it, AI will dump 8 questions at you and you'll feel overwhelmed and answer none of them well.

## Three flavors

### Teacher — a class management decision

```
I want to think out loud about a decision. Don't give me the answer.

The decision is: whether to move Yousef out of my 4th period class.
He's disruptive but I'm not sure if removing him is fair or if I'm just tired.
What I'm leaning toward: keeping him, with a behavior contract.
What's making me hesitate: the rest of the class is suffering and I'm
exhausted by Friday.

Ask me one question at a time.
```

### NGO assistant — a program decision

```
I want to think out loud about a decision. Don't give me the answer.

The decision is: whether to expand our literacy program to a second district
next year, or deepen it in the current one.
What I'm leaning toward: expanding, because the funder wants growth.
What's making me hesitate: we don't have the staff, and outcomes in the
current district are still mediocre.

Ask me one question at a time.
```

### Business — a strategic decision

```
I want to think out loud about a decision. Don't give me the answer.

The decision is: whether to drop my lowest-paying client tier and focus
only on premium clients.
What I'm leaning toward: dropping them.
What's making me hesitate: it's 40% of my revenue and I'm scared of the gap.

Ask me one question at a time.
```

Run yours. Go through at least 4 question-and-answer rounds before stopping.

## Asking for devil's advocate

Once you have a position, stress-test it.

```
Here's the conclusion I'm coming to: [your conclusion].

Now play devil's advocate. Argue the opposite case as strongly as you can.
What am I not seeing? Where is my reasoning weak?
Don't be polite. Don't hedge.
```

You'll get a list of counter-arguments. Some will hit. Some won't. The ones that hit are gold — that's the part of the situation you were avoiding.

---

## When to trust AI's pushback and when to ignore it

AI's pushback is useful when:

- It's about logic and consistency. ("You said X is the priority but you're spending most of your time on Y.")
- It's surfacing a tradeoff you hadn't named.
- It's asking you to define a vague term you keep using.

AI's pushback is NOT useful when:

- It's about your specific people, your culture, your relationships. AI doesn't know your colleague Maria or your son's teacher.
- It's making moral judgments that depend on context it doesn't have.
- It's confidently stating "research shows..." without giving you a source.

A good rule — if AI says "you should do X," ignore it. If AI says "have you considered why you keep avoiding X?" — pay attention.

## The honesty multiplier

AI conversations get dramatically more useful when you're honest about the messy parts.

Vague version: "I'm thinking about leaving this job."

Honest version: "I'm thinking about leaving this job. I tell people it's because of growth, but actually I can't stand my manager and I'm scared no other job will pay as well."

The second version produces a useful conversation. The first produces career-coach platitudes.

The chat doesn't go anywhere. You can write things you wouldn't say out loud.

---

## Knowing when to stop

You can talk to AI forever. At some point more questions stop adding clarity and start adding noise.

Signs you should stop:

- You've started repeating yourself
- You feel calmer but you keep typing anyway
- You know the answer but you don't want to face it
- It's been 45 minutes

When you stop, write down in one sentence what you decided or what you noticed. The whole conversation is worth nothing if you don't extract that one line.

---

## A warning

AI will rarely tell you that you're wrong about something deeply personal. It tilts toward being agreeable. If you want real pushback, you have to ask for it explicitly, and even then it'll soften the blow.

For decisions that really matter — career, family, money, health — use AI to think, but get at least one human you trust to weigh in too. Friends notice things AI can't.

## Homework

1. Pick a decision you've been circling for more than a week. Run the "help me think" prompt with voice mode if you can. Go at least 5 question rounds.
2. After the conversation, write down in ONE sentence what you actually decided or noticed. Save it somewhere you'll see in a week.
3. Try the devil's advocate prompt on a position you hold strongly. Notice which counter-arguments sting (those are the real ones).
4. Bring to next session — one thing AI helped you see, and one thing it got wrong that a human friend would have caught.
