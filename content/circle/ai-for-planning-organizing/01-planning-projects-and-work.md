# Planning Projects and Work with AI

Most plans fail not because the goal was wrong, but because nobody broke it down honestly. You said "I'll launch the after-school program by September" and never wrote down the twenty smaller things that have to happen first. AI is genuinely useful here — not because it knows your situation, but because it forces you to lay out the steps you've been avoiding.

This lesson is about turning a fuzzy goal into a real plan with phases, effort estimates, and a list of things that could go wrong.

---

## What AI is actually good at in planning

AI is good at:

- Listing the obvious steps you forgot
- Suggesting a phase structure (week 1, week 2, etc.)
- Surfacing risks and dependencies
- Reformatting your messy thoughts into a clean outline

AI is bad at:

- Knowing how long things really take in your context
- Understanding office politics, family obligations, or real budgets
- Telling you what actually matters most to you

A useful way to think about it — AI gives you a first draft of a plan. You are the editor.

## The core prompt: from goal to plan

Here is the prompt we'll all use, with our own goals. Copy this, then change the bracketed parts.

```
I want to plan: [describe your goal in 1-2 sentences].

The deadline is [date or "flexible"].
The people involved are [me / me + 2 others / etc.].
My constraints are [budget, time per week, anything else].

Please:
1. Break this into 3-5 phases with rough time estimates.
2. For each phase, list the concrete tasks.
3. Flag the 3 biggest risks or things likely to go wrong.
4. Tell me what I should decide BEFORE starting.

Ask me clarifying questions if anything is unclear.
```

That last line matters. Without it, AI will just guess and produce a confident-sounding plan based on nothing.

---

## Three flavors of the same prompt

### Teacher — a lesson plan for a new unit

```
I want to plan a 4-week unit on environmental science for 9th graders.
The deadline is the start of next term (3 weeks from now).
The people involved are me and one co-teacher.
My constraints are: 2 hours of prep per week, no field trip budget, mixed-ability class of 28.

Please break this into phases, list tasks, flag risks,
and tell me what to decide before I start.
```

### NGO assistant — a community project

```
I want to plan a literacy workshop series for 30 mothers in our district.
The deadline is end of October (4 months away).
The people involved are me, the program director, and 2 volunteer facilitators.
My constraints are: $800 budget, venue is donated but only available evenings,
most participants have young children.

Please break this into phases, list tasks, flag risks,
and tell me what to decide before I start.
```

### Business — a small launch

```
I want to plan the launch of a new service offering (1-on-1 consulting packages)
for my existing clients.
The deadline is end of next quarter.
The people involved are me and a part-time assistant.
My constraints are: I can spend 5 hours a week on this, no paid ads budget,
my list has 400 past clients.

Please break this into phases, list tasks, flag risks,
and tell me what to decide before I start.
```

Run yours now. Read the result out loud to the group.

## The optimism problem

Here is the thing nobody tells you. AI plans are almost always too optimistic.

It will say "Week 1: research and outline." Research and outline takes three weeks in real life because you got sick, your kid was home, the venue cancelled, and you spent two days arguing about the title.

A simple fix — after AI gives you a plan, paste it back with this:

```
Look at the plan you just gave me. Where is it being optimistic?
What real-world things tend to make plans like this slip?
Give me a more honest version with buffer time.
```

You'll often get a noticeably better plan on the second pass. Always do the second pass.

---

## Asking about risks specifically

Risks are the part most people skip. AI is actually decent at this if you ask directly.

```
For the plan above, list the 5 things most likely to derail it.
For each one, give me an early warning sign I can watch for,
and one thing I can do now to reduce the risk.
```

Teacher example — "Students may not have internet at home" is a risk. Early warning sign — first homework completion rate. Action now — survey families before assigning the first online task.

---

## When to stop planning and start

A trap with AI is endless re-planning. You can ask it to refine the plan forever, and it will happily oblige. At some point the plan is good enough and the only thing left is to do the thing.

Rule of thumb — two passes with AI, then start working from the plan. Adjust as you go.

## Homework

1. Pick one real goal you've been putting off. Use the core prompt above with your real constraints. Save the output.
2. Do the "where is this being optimistic" follow-up. Compare the two versions and notice what changed.
3. From the final plan, write down the SINGLE next concrete action you can take this week. One action. Not five.
4. Bring the plan to next session and tell the group one thing AI got right and one thing it got wrong about your situation.
