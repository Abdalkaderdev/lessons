# Cognitive Biases That Cost You Money

Your brain evolved to keep you alive in a hostile environment, not to make rational decisions about probability under stress. Markets exploit the gap. Every losing trader you've ever met was running the same handful of mental bugs. Naming them is half the cure.

---

## FOMO — Fear of Missing Out

You see a chart that's already up 8% on the day. You weren't in it. You feel a sick pull to "get in before it's too late." You buy the top. It rolls over. You're now the bag holder.

**FOMO is always loudest at the top.** By the time a move is obvious enough to make you panic about missing it, the people who had an edge are already selling to you.

> If you have to rush, you've already lost.

---

## Revenge trading

You take a loss. Your brain frames it as an injustice. You open the platform again — not because there's a setup, but because you need to "get it back." You size up. You enter without a plan. You lose more. Now you're in a hole and your judgment is wrecked.

**Example:** Fawzi loses $200 on a clean stop-out. Instead of walking away, he opens a smaller-cap, more volatile coin he doesn't understand and triples his usual size to "make it back fast." He loses $600 in 20 minutes.

The market doesn't owe you anything back. It doesn't even know you exist.

---

## Confirmation bias

You decide a stock is going up. From that moment on, you only see news that supports it. The bullish tweets feel insightful. The bearish ones feel stupid. You ignore the volume drying up. You ignore the lower high. You're not analyzing anymore — you're defending.

**Fix:** Before every trade, write down what would prove you wrong. If you can't name the disqualifier, you don't have a thesis. You have a wish.

> A real thesis includes the line "I am wrong if ___."

---

## Recency bias

The last three trades worked. You assume the next one will too, so you size up. Or the last three lost, so you assume your strategy is broken, and you abandon it right before the win that would've recouped everything.

**Markets mean-revert. Your confidence shouldn't.** A 6-trade sample tells you almost nothing about whether your edge is real. A 200-trade sample starts to.

---

## Loss aversion — the killer

This one is the most expensive bias in trading. Research shows losses feel roughly twice as painful as equivalent gains feel good. So traders do two destructive things:

- **Cut winners short.** A trade is up 1R. You're scared it'll come back. You take profit. Then it runs to 5R without you.
- **Let losers run.** A trade is down 1R. Taking the loss hurts. So you "give it room." It goes to 3R. Now closing it hurts triple. You freeze. It goes to 5R. Account damaged.

You end up with a portfolio of small wins and huge losses — the exact inverse of a profitable trader.

> Amateurs cut winners and hold losers. Professionals do the opposite. That's the whole game.

## Use AI to...

Audit your last 20 trades for which biases are eating you alive:

```text
Here are my last 20 trades with entry, exit, planned stop, planned target, actual exit reason, and a short note on how I felt:

[paste table]

For each trade, identify which cognitive bias (FOMO, revenge, confirmation, recency, loss aversion, or none) most likely influenced it. Then rank the biases from most to least costly for me. Don't be polite — show me the pattern.
```

Pre-trade reality check, used right before entering:

```text
I'm about to take this trade: [setup, entry, stop, target, size, reasoning]. Play devil's advocate. What would a bearish trader see in this same chart? What would prove my thesis wrong? Am I rushing, revenge trading, or chasing? Be blunt.
```

## Homework

1. Pick the one bias from this list that hits you hardest. Write a sentence describing the last time it cost you money. Tape it next to your screen.
2. For your next 10 trades, write down before entry: "I am wrong if ___." If you can't fill it in, don't take the trade.
3. Track every trade where you exited early out of fear or held past your stop out of hope. After 2 weeks, calculate: how much did those two behaviors alone cost you?
